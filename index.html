Why real-robot VLA deployments fail

Michael Lai
September 2026

A checkpoint can restore cleanly, return finite actions, and still be nearly useless on hardware. In our deployments, the failure was rarely “the model did not load.” It was usually a smaller mismatch: one camera role, one unit, one timing assumption, one start pose, or one recorder label.

Over two months of working across GR3, dual-PiPER X, and Astribot S1, the same pattern kept showing up. The runtime was guessing details that the training data had already fixed. Once those details drifted, adding more smoothing or relaxing a watchdog only made the real cause harder to see.

This is a field account of those failures and the deployment structure that came out of them: establish the raw policy first, measure timing before tuning RTC, choose motion processing for a specific artifact, and make DAgger collection reproduce the original training contract.

01 · RUNTIME CONTRACT

A matching tensor shape is not a runtime contract

A checkpoint can restore cleanly, accept a synthetic observation, and return a finite [32,14] tensor while still being incompatible with the robot. Shape validation proves memory layout. It does not prove meaning.

Two tensors with identical shapes can encode different camera roles, field orders, units, timestamps, or action semantics. Those mismatches often survive every offline assertion and only appear as weak, erratic, or unsafe behavior on hardware.

“Three RGB cameras plus a 14-dimensional state produces 14-dimensional actions” is an interface summary, not a deployment contract. The enforceable contract must answer:

  • Which camera is left? The robot's left, or left in the operator's video wall?
  • RGB or BGR, and who does the conversion — driver, graph, or adapter?
  • Is the gripper in metres, millimetres, or percent of stroke?
  • Does row 0 of the chunk mean "right now" or "the next control tick"?
  • Which exact prompt strings did it see? Not "close the laptop" — "Close the laptop.", capital C, full stop.

If any answer is missing, the checkpoint is not ready for robot-facing qualification.

Make the checkpoint declare its contract

Every deployable bundle ships a frozen, machine-readable contract.json that the runtime enforces. The following example is shortened for readability:

model-store/models/<policy>/<deployment>/<checkpoint>/contract.json 
{
  "observation": {
    "head_rgb":        "uint8[H,W,3] RGB",
    "left_wrist_rgb":  "uint8[H,W,3] RGB",
    "right_wrist_rgb": "uint8[H,W,3] RGB",
    "state":           "float32[14] absolute joints/grippers",
    "prompt":          "one canonical atomic-task instruction"
  },
  "action": {
    "representation": "absolute joint/gripper targets after output transform",
    "units":          "joint radians; gripper width in metres",
    "shape":          [32, 14],
    "control_hz":     30,
    "execute_chunk":  16
  },
  "canonical_prompts": [ "Close the laptop.", "Pick up one pen and place it in the pen holder.", ... ],

  "evidence_scope": {
    "establishes":        ["bundle restore", "finite synthetic inference", "I/O contract"],
    "does_not_establish": ["simulator success", "real robot success", "task success rate"]
  }
}

The evidence_scope block is important: the bundle records what qualification proves and what remains unverified. A successful restore and finite inference do not claim real-robot task success.

Field order is part of the contract

The dual-PiPER state contains two 6-DoF arms and two grippers, but it is not ordered as 12 arm joints followed by two grippers:

real_world/robots/piper_x_dual/mapping.py 
ACTION_NAMES = (
    left_joint1 … left_joint6,   left_gripper,     # indices 0-6
    right_joint1 … right_joint6, right_gripper,    # indices 7-13
)
units = ("rad",)*6 + ("m",) + ("rad",)*6 + ("m",)

The grippers are interleaved, at index 6 and index 13. Assume the intuitive layout and every right-arm joint slides over by one, a number in metres lands in a slot that expects radians, and the robot does something you will remember for a while. The dimension check passes the entire time, because the shape is still [14].

This is why our schemas carry fields, units and semantics as parallel tuples instead of a bare dimension, and why both ends compare a schema_id before anything moves. A 15 Hz chunk also can't quietly execute at 30 Hz — frequency is checked separately, because we got that wrong too.

Apply color conversion exactly once

Our capture profile publishes bgr8. The bundle contract says RGB. Somebody has to swap the channels exactly once, in a place you can point at. Two conversions cancel out. Zero conversions give the model a perfectly plausible image of a world with the wrong colours, and it behaves oddly in a way no assertion will ever catch.

A physical check software cannot replace

Our first left/right camera labelling on the PiPER cell was simply wrong. Both streams healthy, both serials pinned in the instance profile, both roles stable. Everything green. Nothing in software could distinguish it from correct.

It was caught by a person standing in front of one camera and waving.

Acceptance now includes an occlusion check per camera, because a camera identity is a physical claim and only a physical test can falsify it. Relabelling in the UI until the picture "looks right" gets you a system that lies consistently, which is strictly worse than one that lies obviously.

02 · BASELINE

First, make the plain policy move

Do not begin qualification with asynchronous prefetch, RTC transitions, and low-pass filtering enabled together. That configuration hides which boundary introduced the failure.

Make the plain policy work before adding optional motion processing. A policy that is slightly jittery but directionally correct provides a usable baseline. A heavily processed policy that does not move cannot tell you whether the fault is in the model, scheduler, filter, safety gate, or hardware path.

For this handbook, raw baseline means:

  • ACTION_RTC_MODE=disabled — no prefetch learning, no blending.
  • --no-model-filter — no low-pass on model joints.
  • Execute prefix at the descriptor default, 16 of a 32-row horizon. The reviewed value, not an experiment.
  • --no-write first. Shadow mode. Nothing reaches the SDK.

What does not get turned off, ever: schema validation, frequency agreement, generation fencing, freshness checks, and the robot-owned final gate with exactly one process allowed to call the SDK writer. Those aren't "processing", they're the floor.

One subtlety that took us a while to internalise: with RTC disabled, elapsed-action alignment still runs. It's mandatory, not an RTC feature. A late chunk from a previous episode still gets discarded, and a reply still gets aligned to the actions consumed while it was in flight. More on why in a second.

Measure raw action dynamics

Not "does the motion look smooth". We compute the per-step maximum absolute joint change across the chunk, and compare its distribution against per-joint velocity percentiles from the original training episodes.

δ= maxj | at+1,j − at,j |
δ

at,j

maxj

That one number separates three failures that feel completely identical on the robot. Too small and the model is timid, or its corrections were collected slowly (§6). Too large and the safety layer is about to rate-limit most of it, so the robot will look damped and behind. Discontinuous and you have a seam, a unit error, or a genuinely unstable region of the policy.

Two real numbers to calibrate against. On GR3, a checkpoint that "felt sluggish" was emitting a median max joint step of 0.041 rad where the checkpoint everyone liked emitted 0.064 rad — 37% smaller, at identical inference latency. In a different run, raw steps of 1.13–1.19 rad hit a 0.10 rad/step limiter and got up to 97 values clipped in a single chunk.

Both of those get reported to us as "the robot is slow". Opposite causes, opposite fixes.

03 · TIMING AND RTC

Timing failures often look like bad motion

“Sluggish” is only a visual symptom. It can mean undersized model steps, queue starvation, repeated trajectory replacement, safety clipping, stale observations, or genuine inference delay. Each cause requires a different fix.

The GR3 pick-doll run from 12 August is a useful example. The checkpoint family was unchanged, but motion became hesitant and alternated between pauses and short bursts.

The first hardware and compute hypotheses did not match the logs:

  • Inference got slower? 73 ms. Same as the run that felt great.
  • Workstation thrashing? 102 GiB of RAM free.
  • GPU cooking? 36 °C.
  • Motors overheating? Robot computer at ~20 °C, and no thermal, over-current or motor-fault entries anywhere in the controller logs.
  • Storage or cache pressure? Clearing 7.6 GB of package caches produced no change.

The workstation and robot controller were healthy. The failure was in scheduling and action admission.

Observed causes

Three things stacked, in descending order of blame.

One: the launch overrode RTC_TRIGGER_STEPS=24, where the qualified value was 22. With a 30-row aligned queue, trigger 24 replans after only 6–8 executed rows—about 0.2–0.27 seconds—and blends 4 of them. Roughly two-thirds of every update interval was therefore inside a transition.

Two: mid-rollout, the checkpoint proposed a right-index seam of 0.623 rad against a 0.56 rad admission limit. The chunk was rejected, and the queue then lost its tail for four consecutive chunks. That starvation produced the observed pause–move–pause motion.

Three: after the rejection, raw model steps reached 1.13–1.19 rad. Safety capped them at 0.10 rad/step and rate-limited up to 97 values per chunk. Heavy limiting looks exactly like "damped and sluggish" to a human watching.

None of that is a hardware problem, and none of it would have been findable without the alignment evidence every accepted chunk publishes — measured delay, aligned offset, queue depth, seam size before and after the transition.

Elapsed-action alignment is mandatory

At 30 Hz, one control step is 33.3 ms. A model may predict 32 rows (1.07 s), while the deployment queues only its validated 16-row execution prefix (0.53 s). RTC operates on that effective execution queue, not on rows the runtime already removed. Measured inference of 70–75 ms consumes roughly 2–3 control steps.

So if inference blocks the control loop, the robot pauses 2–3 ticks between chunks and you can hear it. If inference runs asynchronously but the reply is installed starting at row 0, you replay 2–3 actions whose moment has already passed, and the robot lurches backwards before going forwards. Async inference fixes the first problem. Alignment fixes the second, which is why it isn't optional.

RTC terminology

Client-side RTC aligns and optionally blends completed action chunks. That's it. Previous-action conditioning, frozen diffusion steps, gradient guidance — those are capabilities of the model backend, and turning on prefetch grants you none of them.

They can also be trained in; our store has a bundle literally named …-trainrtc-d0to6-v1. That's a property of those weights, and still a third separate thing from what real_world/rtc.py does. We have watched all three get called "RTC" in the same conversation.

04 · MOTION CONDITIONING

Smoothing is not one thing

Smoothing is not one operation. Chunk transitions, low-pass filtering, splines, and rate limits act on different failure modes and at different locations in the control path.

We compared against DAgger-GR3, the previous-generation stack, which everyone agreed felt smoother. It does less smoothing. Substantially less. It executes an entire coherent chunk — roughly 1–1.7 seconds of one consistent plan — before requesting the next. And it allows 0.15 rad per live joint step where ours allows 0.10.

So the newer stack, with more filtering and tighter limits, looked worse.

A stack that replaces its trajectory more often and clips its catch-up motion can look less smooth than one that commits to a coherent plan. Every replan introduces another boundary; every clip increases tracking lag.

The fix we recommended was not "loosen the safety cap". It was RTC_TRIGGER_STEPS=16 — about 14 coherent rows between accepted plans instead of 6 — with every limit left exactly where it was.

Processors active in the qualified runtime

Only three motion-processing mechanisms are active in the qualified configuration:

  • A first-order low-pass on model joints. On by default for dual-PiPER, α = 0.6.
  • RTC alignment, and optionally a bounded blend. Align on GR3 and Astribot; blend is opt-in.
  • Quintic minimum-jerk staging for Prepare, Home and dataset-pose transitions.

PCHIP resampling and a chunk QP both exist in real_world/output.py. Both default to off, GR3 ships GR3_CHUNK_SMOOTHING=off as its committed default, and we have never qualified either onto hardware. I mention them mainly so that when you read that file you don't assume they're load-bearing.

Low-pass behavior and filter state

yt= yt−1 +α ( xt− yt−1 ) , 0<α≤1
yt
the filtered target actually sent this tick
yt−1

xt

α
0.6 on dual-PiPER. Smaller is smoother and laggier

The filter is stateful, and that state is the whole story. If the target sits at 1.0 and the output reads 0.5, that's not a reduced goal — the next updates keep converging toward 1.0. Fine in theory. But on a finite action chunk, replanning usually arrives before convergence does, so the real behaviour is closer to "permanently 60% of the way there". Which is not what anyone signed up for.

Four wiring details, each of which we learned by breaking it:

  • It runs after RTC coordination, on model joints only.
  • It re-initialises from fresh measured joints on the first step after Start, takeover, HOLD, feedback failure or generation change.
  • It is not reset by ordinary chunk replacement. Resetting there re-introduces exactly the seam the filter exists to smooth.
  • Expert actions and both gripper widths bypass it entirely. Phase lag on a gripper misses the grasp, and there is no value of α that fixes that.

The unfiltered target gets checked first, and the filtered target passes the final safety gate again before the owner writes. Filtering is not a way around the gate.

05 · INITIAL STATE

The initial pose belongs to the data distribution

A geometrically tidy pose is not automatically a valid policy start state. On GR3, forward kinematics showed the left hand about 24 mm farther forward than the right and the left index tip about 8 mm higher, so the team created a more symmetric initialization.

So the initialisation profile moved from …start-v5-claude-elbows to …start-v6-bilateral-fk, symmetrising both arms by moving several joints up to 0.084 rad (4.8°).

The geometry got better. The behaviour got worse.

The cause was visible in the dataset: v6 is a computed bilateral-FK pose, not a frame from a training episode. The checkpoint therefore received an out-of-distribution silhouette on its first observation. The resulting hesitation initially looked like a timing problem.

A per-joint median has the same defect. Every individual joint value may be typical while their combination forms a body configuration that appears in zero episodes. Select complete recorded states, not synthetic combinations of marginal statistics.

Select complete poses from recorded starts

The PiPER runtime instead selects complete recorded frames: twelve follower-state medoids per prompt, drawn from training-candidate episodes. Each candidate must satisfy:

  • Anchored 8 frames (~267 ms at 30 Hz) before detected sustained joint-motion onset — the moment just before the demonstrator committed.
  • Strictly inside the first 22% of the episode. Never mid-rollout, never the tail.
  • At least 120 following frames with significant future motion, so it's a genuine beginning and not a pause.
  • Far from any terminal state. Terminal and post-completion frames are forbidden by the schema outright.
  • A prompt with no library entry fails closed, rather than falling back to some generic home pose.

At runtime the sole hardware owner only accepts a pose id from a SHA-256-bound library, then independently re-derives joint limits and maximum displacement before executing anything. The UI never sends a raw 12-D target. If the current position can't reach the candidate directly, the owner routes through a declared clear/home pose as two bounded segments, checking interpolated flange height before each dispatch — and if that bounded path still isn't safe, it fails closed rather than opening a gate.

Limitation

The existing demonstrations mostly share one starting region. These medoids are valid early-episode variants, not evidence of a diverse start distribution. Real coverage requires demonstrations collected from varied starts.

06 · DAGGER / HITL DATA

Why DAgger data can make a policy worse

DAgger data is useful only when its observation, action, timing, and labeling semantics match the policy that will consume it. A successful expert takeover can still produce harmful training data when those contracts drift.

In the older GR3 recorder, six independent issues compounded into worse fine-tuning performance:

These faults belonged to the older GR3 recorder and are now fixed. Their failure patterns remain relevant because four of the six are invisible without explicit data audits.

  1. Later corrections were silently dropped. The recorder cleared its episode_index after each save or discard, and refused to start without one — but the controller only emitted that index once, when the model episode began. So the first takeover in an episode recorded fine. The second one didn't. Our dataset was systematically biased toward first corrections, which are usually the least interesting ones.
  2. label.valid was polluted. Repeated takeover-state messages emitted a default decision with label_valid=false. In the raw collection: 88,825 false against 77,354 true validity events, with about 20% of aligned expert actions having a false event as their nearest sample. Our strict converter didn't naively consume that stream, so the bundle survived — but anything joining by nearest timestamp would have inherited it wholesale.
  3. "Success" just meant the operator pressed save. Not that the doll ended up anywhere in particular.
  4. We only recorded the takeover fragment. No model failure immediately before, no completed recovery after. Isolated corrections with the task context sliced off.
  5. The mixture over-sampled corrections badly. New DAgger data was 28 of 64 samples per batch — 43.75% — and each fragment was seen roughly 8.3 times by step 4000. Short, salient correction motions overwrite the long successful behaviour the parent model already had.
  6. Six recordings had safety-limited actions paired with unclipped labels. Quarantined in the strict rebuild.

For scale, the strict set came out at 22,996 frames / 13,448 valid H32 queries. The later hybrid bundle reported 211 DAgger segments, 22,742 frames, 16,201 valid queries, at roughly 40% DAgger / 60% replay.

Join streams on control ticks

Cameras run at 30 Hz. The hardware controller runs at 60, 100 or 200. On GR3 we measured robot state at ≈30.29 Hz, expert actions at ≈99.88 Hz, alignment normally inside 5 ms. Those are good numbers. They are still not good enough to join by nearest timestamp, because "nearest" can silently cross a takeover boundary or pick up the wrong validity heartbeat.

So identity is allocated causally instead: the sole owner allocates control_tick_id only after a successful SDK dispatch. The executed action, the selected source, the label validity and the accepted evidence for that dispatch all share that one id. Router-side proposal ids are kept separately as decision_control_tick_id and are explicitly not treated as proof that hardware moved. Anything missing, conflicting, or only joinable by timestamp fallback goes to quarantine — never guessed into a valid label.

Which brings up the other thing worth separating. There are five different tensors in this pipeline that people all call "the action":

A row is trainable only if source = EXPERT, takeover references were valid, safety was valid, and label.valid = true. The label itself is action.expert_aligned_unclipped_robot — the operator's intended, aligned target.

The unclipped intent is a cleaner label only when the robot executed essentially the same target. If safety materially changed it, training on intent teaches behavior that was never demonstrated in the environment. Those recordings are quarantined rather than repaired because the intended label cannot be reconstructed reliably.

Align takeover transitions explicitly

On the rising edge of takeover we capture two configurations exactly once — the measured robot configuration and the expert device configuration. Everything after that is relative:

aexpert(t)= q0robot + ( qexo(t) − q0exo )
aexpert(t)
the aligned expert target, and the training label
q0robot
measured robot configuration at the takeover edge
qexo(t)
the exoskeleton's current configuration
q0exo
the exoskeleton's configuration at that same edge

Without it, the robot jumps toward the exoskeleton's completely unrelated absolute configuration the instant you take over. With it, what transfers is the operator's motion, which is what you wanted to record in the first place.

Expert speed becomes a dataset property

The QNexo adapter defaulted to EXO_WAIST_YAW_SPEED = 0.1, then divided that value by 100: 0.001 rad per update, or about 0.1 rad/s at the intended 100 Hz loop. The resulting expert corrections were visibly slow.

Raising it to 0.5 by feel makes teleoperation much nicer, and simultaneously changes the correction distribution the next checkpoint learns from. Because the model predicts future target positions rather than velocities, slower labels just mean smaller target changes over the H32 horizon. And where the same visual and state situations carry both fast pretraining labels and slow DAgger labels, the loss cheerfully averages them into hesitancy.

The properly calibrated move is to compute per-joint velocity and acceleration percentiles from the original episodes and match those. Also: don't try to repair old slow data by multiplying the yaw values. It breaks temporal and arm/waist coordination and produces labels that are individually plausible and jointly impossible.

Encode camera streams as video

Numeric, event and alignment metadata belong in Parquet. Camera frames do not, and the arithmetic isn't close. One real 30 Hz dual-PiPER episode, three 1280×720 RGB streams stored as Parquet arrays:

Camera streams: 4.59 GB. All 14 numeric / event / label streams combined: 0.59 MB. Episode duration: 52.6 s.

Which is exactly what you'd predict — 3 × 1280 × 720 × 3 bytes is 2.76 MB per frame, times 30 Hz is 82.9 MB/s, times 52.6 s is 4.36 GB. Parquet compression does essentially nothing to camera-noise-bearing uint8, so images end up roughly 7,700× the size of everything else and no compression setting will change that ratio. Encode to H.264 MP4 and keep the frame → timestamp → control_tick_id mapping in the tabular metadata, so the causal joins still work.

07 · MODEL ONBOARDING

A checkpoint should need a descriptor, not a new launcher

Deployment becomes repeatable when every checkpoint-specific question has one machine-readable source of truth.

The rule we converged on is that a new checkpoint should need a descriptor, not a new branch in the launcher. Everything the launcher needs to know about a specific model is data. The launcher itself contains no if model == ….

policy/<adapter>/deployments/<deployment>.yml  ·  abridged 
bundle:                          # identity and immutability
  default_path: model-store/models/<policy>/<deployment>/<checkpoint>
  required:     [checkpoint/model.safetensors, contract.json]
  verifier:     policy/<adapter>/verify_bundle.py

policy:                          # timing contract, mirrored from contract.json
  action_frequency_hz:   30
  action_horizon:        32
  default_execute_chunk: 16
  default_execute_mode:  auto    # follow the model's execute_prefix_steps

observation:
  camera_profile: camera_profiles/piper-x-dual-rgb-1280x720-30.yml

server:
  default_mode: managed          # launcher owns the PolicyServer lifecycle

tasks:
  catalog_path: contract.json    # authority is the bundle, not this file

intake:
  provider:       aliyun_oss
  expected_bytes: FROM_PUBLISHER_MANIFEST

qualification:
  verifier_args: [--full-manifest]
  tests:         [ tests/test_inference.py, …, tests/test_robot_contract.py ]

Read it top to bottom and you can answer: what weights, verified how, at what rate, with which cameras, served how, through which graph, with which prompts, from where, proved by which tests. That's the whole idea.

Two commands cover the lifecycle — onboard for first-time intake, qualify for an installed runtime. Same stage machine: verify every file against three manifests (local hashes, local manifest, publisher's manifest), build an isolated model runtime, run the descriptor's own test list, bind a free loopback port, boot the managed server, probe it with a contract-shaped synthetic observation and assert a finite [32,14] at 30 Hz, then do a no-write launcher dry run. Out comes a JSON report and numbered per-stage logs.

Port ownership

Because the tempting response to address already in use is to kill everything holding the port, and we have watched that go badly. The correct response is to identify the listener and validate its WebSocket HELLO: if the deployment identity matches, reuse it deliberately; if it doesn't, stop only the process the launcher owns.

A stray server from a different checkpoint that happens to answer on the right port will serve you confident, wrong actions all afternoon, and nothing in your logs will look alarming.

The central ownership invariant is strict: exactly one process may call the SDK. Model and expert actions are proposals. The router selects a source, the robot-owned gate admits or holds it, the owner writes, and the recorder observes without hardware authority.

The VLM sits above all of this, and that placement is deliberate. Task selection and recovery reasoning is slow and fallible; motor control is hard real-time. Wire them together directly and a two-second language-model round trip ends up inside a 33-millisecond control loop.

The supervisor is not a second Dora control graph. It communicates through an authenticated loopback API, may emit only prompts from the checkpoint’s declared catalogue, and never emits joint targets. The sole writer counts its rollout budget from model actions actually received. Once execution starts, the real-time path does not include the VLM.

08 · FAULT ISOLATION

Follow the first broken boundary

Debug from the first invalid boundary, not from the robot’s final visual symptom. The table below lists the highest-yield checks in field order.

The underlying move in all of these is the same: don't debug from the final visual symptom. Follow one observation and one action through the system and find the first boundary where the values stopped matching their contract. Five questions, in order — what did the model receive, what did it return, what did the runtime schedule, what did the owner accept, what physically happened. Most arguments happen because somebody jumped from question one straight to question five.

Keep these five distinctions explicit

Each of these, when it collapses in someone's head or in a doc, produces a specific class of bug that is miserable to find — because everyone involved is using the same word.

  • Current vs historical. Current code is the source of truth. A chat thread from August is a case study about a system that may have changed since. The recorder bugs in §6 were real and are fixed; what transfers is the shape of the failure.
  • Client RTC vs model-native guidance. Covered above. Prefetch grants you nothing about conditioning.
  • Raw vs coordinated vs executed. Three different tensors. Once blending is on, the coordinated stream is not byte-for-byte the raw response, and labelling it "raw" in an evidence pipeline poisons everything downstream of it.
  • Raw episode vs derived view. The full recording keeps context; the training view is a strict projection of it. Keep both, immutably, so a labelling decision can be re-derived rather than argued about.
  • Qualification vs authorisation. A green report proves restore, contract shape, service parity and a no-write dry run. It says nothing about task success — the bundle's own evidence_scope.does_not_establish says so out loud. Moving a real robot needs an explicit live-run request, the write gate, and a supervised canary with a person on the E-stop.

Dataset-first debugging

The consistent result is operational rather than novel: when deployment behavior becomes difficult to explain, return to the dataset and training contract before adding runtime corrections.

Sample the starting pose from the data. Measure expert speed from the data. Recover camera role and timing from the data. Trace action semantics back to the transform that produced the data.

The training contract obviously won't fix a disconnected CAN bus or a dead camera. What it does is stop a hardware fault from being mistaken for a model fault, and stop an out-of-distribution deployment from getting "fixed" with runtime tricks that quietly poison the next round of training.

Open limitations remain. The demonstrations do not contain broad start-pose diversity, medoid selection cannot create missing coverage, the 40/60 DAgger replay ratio is empirical rather than derived, and a single rollout cannot distinguish variance from a systematic policy defect.

09 · HARDWARE RUNBOOKS

GR3 and PiPER fail in different ways

GR3 and dual-PiPER fail at different physical boundaries. GR3 is a load-bearing humanoid: support and standing state come first. PiPER X is fixed-base, but a disabled arm can still fall under gravity: identity, CAN feedback and servo enable state come first.

Load-bearing humanoid GR3 runbook ↓ Lift support, foot loading, PdStand and the Aurora controller-state chain. Fixed-base dual arm Dual-PiPER X runbook ↓ Two CAN identities, six enable bits per arm, three physical camera roles and gravity-safe recovery.
GR3

Support and balance are the safety boundary.

A running policy process does not prove that GR3 is carrying its own weight. Keep the lift attached, prove the standing controller from readback, and only then hand the declared joints to policy control.

01LiftRobot fully supported; fall area and cables clear.
02StandStanding mode confirmed from controller readback.
03LowerBoth soles placed flat with small lift movements.
04Load checkFloor reaction and active recovery confirmed.
05PolicyOne initialization and exact READY handoff.

Five checks, then run

  1. Lift first. Support the full robot. Clear the feet, cables and fall area; keep the E-stop reachable.
  2. Confirm standing mode. Wait for the controller’s actual state readback — a running process alone proves nothing.
  3. Lower slowly. Use small lift movements until both soles sit flat. Stop if either foot folds, slides or catches a cable.
  4. Feel the load. Gently guide the pelvis backward until the floor pushes back and you feel an upward reaction. With the lift still ready, use one small push at the pelvis to confirm the robot actively recovers.
  5. Start the policy. Initialize once, wait for the exact control states and READY message, then run. Do not keep pressing initialize through a fault.
GR3 controller state

AuroraCore → PdStand → i → UserController_A + RemoteState → CONTROLLER ready
UserController_A runs lower-body RL balance/walking; RemoteState gives the declared upper-body joints to the model. AuroraClient only requests and reads these states.

GR3 stop condition

The lift stays attached until the run is over. If state, support or balance is uncertain, stop before starting the policy.

PiPER X × 2

Identity and feedback are the safety boundary.

Each arm has its own SocketCAN bus and each camera has a physical role. A logical name is trusted only after the adapter serial, cable label, feedback and a physical left/right test agree.

If an arm hits the ground and disables

  1. Stop the runtime first. Stop the policy and exit the Dora graph. Confirm the XPolicy PiPER owner is no longer connected. The maintenance UI must never become a second writer beside the runtime.
  2. Remove the physical cause. Keep the E-stop reachable, support the disabled arm against gravity, clear the ground/obstacle contact and inspect the arm, gripper and cables. Do not enable while the arm is pinned against something.
  3. Open the site-approved PiPER maintenance UI. Refresh CAN, select the stable interface for the affected side, activate it at 1 Mbps only if it is not already UP, select the firmware declared by the instance profile, and Connect.
  4. Read status before commanding. Confirm joint feedback and the selected physical side. Click Enable. If Enable is refused because a fault remains latched, keep the arm supported and use Reset once according to the UI/vendor procedure, then Enable again. Do not repeatedly reset through an unexplained fault.
  5. Prove recovery. All six joint-enable indicators must read enabled, feedback must keep updating, and a follower arm must report its CAN-control/follower mode. Do not use Go Zero, Move P/L or trajectory playback as an enable test.
  6. Return ownership. Disconnect and close the maintenance UI. Run the repository’s no-write device verification, then restart the normal launcher. The XPolicy owner must again be the only SDK writer.

Maintenance warning

The maintenance app uses the official pyAgxArm SDK. Its Disable, Reset, role switch, Go Zero and trajectory controls can cause motion or gravity drop. Software emergency stop is not a replacement for the physical E-stop. Use only the minimum recovery controls needed.

Stable CAN identity

Linux enumeration can change after reboot or unplugging. The USB-CAN serial identifies the adapter, and the stable netdev is derived from it. The adapter must remain physically paired and labelled with its arm-side CAN harness because the SDK may not expose a useful unique arm serial. Confirm the pairing with a controlled one-arm disconnect test during commissioning.

Dual-PiPER stop condition

Do not start a policy with one arm disabled, stale or ambiguously identified. Both arm states and all required camera roles must be fresh before the model’s 14-D observation is valid.
